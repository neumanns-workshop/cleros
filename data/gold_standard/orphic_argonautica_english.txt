ORPHIC ARGONAUTICA — ENGLISH TRANSLATION

  1. O lord, ruler of Pytho, Far-shooter, prophet,
  2. who by lot attained the Parnassian crag of the sun-bathed peak,
  3. your excellence I hymn; and do you grant me noble fame;
  4. send upon my breast a truth-declaring voice,
  5. that to far-scattered mortals I may proclaim a clear-voiced song
  6. at the Muse’s behests and with the close-strung pectis.
  7. For now, to you, lyre‑working one, as I sing the beloved lay,
  8. my spirit urges me to tell the very things which never before
  9. I declared when I proclaimed the deeds of Bakchos and of lord Apollon.
 10. Driven on by the goad, I was bringing to light dread afflictions,
 11. remedies for mortal men—and thereafter oaths for initiates;
 12. first of all the implacable Anankē of ancient Chaos.
 13. and Kronos, who engendered beneath boundless birth‑pangs
 14. Aither and the two‑formed, far‑seen, glorious Eros,
 15. renowned father of ever‑born Nyx; whom indeed they call Phanes,
 16. The later‑born mortals call him Phanes; for he first shone forth;
 17. and the child of Brimo of exceeding might, and the baleful deeds
 18. of the Earth‑born, who from Heaven dripped the woeful blood.
 19. the seed of generation, whence aforetime the race sprang forth
 20. of mortals, who ever abide throughout the boundless earth;
 21. and the service of Zeus, and the worship of the Mountain-roaming One.
 22. as in the Kybelian mountains he contrived the Mother’s maiden,
 23. Persephone, in regard to her father, the resistless Kronion;
 24. and the far‑famed wounding by Heracles at the fair‑fruited [garden].
 25. and the oaths of the Idaeans, and the boundless might of the Korybantes.
 26. Demeter’s wandering, and Persephone’s great grief,
 27. and how she was Thesmophoros; and the splendid gifts of the Kabeiroi,
 28. and the ineffable oracles of Nyx concerning lord Bakchos,
 29. and holy Lemnos and sea‑girt Samothrace,
 30. and steep Cyprus, and Adonaean Aphrodite,
 31. the orgies of Praxidikē, and of warlike Night‑Athena,
 32. and the laments of the Egyptians and the sacred pots of Osiris.
 33. And of divination you learned the much-tried pathways,
 34. of wild beasts and of birds, and that which is the disposition of the entrails;
 35. and whatever they foretell on the dream-ways,
 36. the souls of mortal men, their heart smitten by sleep;
 37. the unravellings of signs and portents, and the courses of the stars;
 38. and the pure-working purification, a great boon to those upon the earth;
 39. and propitiations of the gods, and the gifts poured over the dead.
 40. Moreover, I have recounted to you the things I saw and understood,
 41. when I went to Taenarum, the shadowy path within Aïdos,
 42. trusting in my own kithara, for love of my wife;
 43. and how much of the Egyptians’ sacred lore I ferreted out,
 44. having crossed to glorious Memphis and the holy cities
 45. of Apis, which the loud‑rushing Neilos has wreathed about;
 46. all these things you have learned most exactly from my breast.
 47. Now, since the baneful gadfly, air-roving, has flown off,
 48. leaving our body behind and betaking itself to the broad heaven,
 49. you shall learn from our voice all that before I kept concealed.
 50. Thus once he traversed Pieria and the towering peaks of Libethra,
 51. (foremost among heroes and demigods, he crossed over),
 52. beseeching me to become an ally of his homecoming,
 53. with a sea‑faring ship toward the inhospitable tribes of men,
 54. to a wealthy and reckless nation, wherein ruled
 55. Aeëtes, son of Helios, light‑bringer to mortals.
 56. For Pelias quailed before the oracular decrees, lest hereafter
 57. the Aisonid by his hand should cast down his kingly rule.
 58. And within his heart she was plying the track of guile;
 59. for she was enjoining him to bring from the Kolchians the Golden Fleece
 60. to horse-rich Thessaly. And when he heard an out-of-measure voice,
 61. stretching forth his hands he called upon Lady Hērē;
 62. for among the Blessed he was wont to glorify her beyond the rest.
 63. But she attended upon the vows, being indeed full of care;
 64. for above all mortals she admired and was wont to love
 65. the dread-in-might hero, the far-famed son of Aison,
 66. and, having called him, Tritogeneia gave her command.
 67. And for him she first fashioned an oak‑built ship,
 68. which also, beneath fir‑wood oars, the briny depths
 69. was first to traverse, and made a track upon the sea.
 70. But when indeed he had gathered the illustrious kings together,
 71. to horse-rich Thrace hastened godlike Iason,
 72. and he came upon me as I was preparing the cunning-wrought kithara,
 73. so that, singing, I might pour forth for you a honey-voiced song,
 74. and enchant wild beasts, and creeping things, and winged creatures.
 75. But when he entered a much‑loved cavern,
 76. he lifted up a honey‑sweet voice from his shaggy breast;
 77. - "Orpheus, dear son of Kalliope and Oiagros,
 78. ruling in Bistonia over the Ciconians rich in flocks,
 79. hail, since for the first time I arrive at the Haemonian watercourses,
 80. and the Strymonian streams, and toward the sheer glens of Rhodope."
 81. And I, among the Minyans, have inherited blood preeminent above all.
 82. A Thessalian, Aisonides; and I declare myself your guest‑friend.
 83. But, friend, receive me with ready heart and hearken to my word
 84. with gentle ears, and, as I entreat, give heed,
 85. to draw near the inmost recesses of Axenos Pontos and the stout Phasis
 86. with the ship Argo, and to point out the maiden paths of the sea,
 87. dear to the heroes,
 88. who indeed await your chelys-lyre and god-ordained voice,
 89. hoping for common succor in their labors upon the sea.
 90. For indeed they do not purpose to sail toward barbarian peoples
 91. apart from you; for even toward the misty gloom
 92. into the nethermost recess, by supplication to the very base of the earth,
 93. to draw near, alone among men, and to discover a return;
 94. for which cause he undertook a shared distress to gratify the Minyae.
 95. and that renown may be heard among men in generations yet to come.
 96. Him then I, answering with words, addressed:
 97. “Aisonides, why, coaxing me, do you ask me these things?”
 98. so that I might come to the Colchians, well-pleasing to the Minyans,
 99. sailing in the well-benched ship over the wine-dark sea?
100. for already for me there is enough of toils, enough there has been of hardships,
101. to whose boundless land and cities I came,
102. in Egypt and in Libya, revealing to mortals the divine oracles.
103. And she delivered me both from wandering and from the gadfly’s goad,
104. my mother; and then she led me into another house,
105. so that I might attain the term of death amid grievous old age.
106. But there is no escaping what indeed lies appointed by fate.
107. I hasten under the promptings of the Moirai; for not dishonored
108. are the Litai, maidens of Zeus Hikesios; I shall now come
109. as one numbered among the younger kings and the demigods.
110. And then indeed I left the lovely cave and crossed beyond,
111. with my very phorminx, and I came to the Minyae
112. with nimble feet, over the Pagasaean strands.
113. There then the band of the best of the Minyans assembled,
114. upon the narrow sands with a roar, and at the surf of Anauros.
115. But when indeed they perceived me making my way by a trackless path,
116. they rose gladly, and the heart in each rejoiced;
117. but I spoke, questioning the noblest men.
118. First I beheld the might of divine Herakles,
119. whom Alkmene bore to Zeus Kronion, having mingled with him,
120. when Helios was leaving the threefold blaze of Seirios,
121. and long darkness was creeping on from every side;
122. and Tiphys Agniades, steersman of the long ship;
123. who then was upon the Thespian waters of Telmissos,
124. traversing the shore-hugging current among the Siphaean folk;
125. who, amid choppy seas and gleaming squalls,
126. knew to set the ship straight by resourceful craft;
127. and I beheld horse‑taming Kastor and Polydeukes,
128. and Mopsos from Titarēssos, whom, once wedded to Ampyx,
129. Arēgonis bore beneath a Chaonian oak;
130. and Peleus the Aiakid, glorious son of Aigina,
131. who ruled the Dolopes in much‑nourishing Phthia.
132. And thrice have I seated on board the illustrious stock of Hermes,
133. Aithalides, whom far-famed Eupolemeia bore,
134. daughter of Myrmidon, in rocky Alope;
135. and Erytos, and fair Echion, whom once
136. lying beside the Nymph Laothoë, daughter of Menetos, he fathered,
137. the golden‑wanded Argeiphontes, lord of Kyllene.
138. Straightway came the Aktorides and ox‑devouring Koronos.
139. Iphiklos too, illustrious scion of Phylakos, stepped forth in turn.
140. And Boutes, son of Aineias, like unto gold-sworded Phoibos.
141. But Canthus, Abantiades, sailed across from Euboea.
142. whom indeed Moira subdued, and Ananke imposed his doom.
143. to perish off Libya, and to be bereft of the homecoming to his house.
144. And Phaleros, son of Alkon, from the streams of Aisepos,
145. came, who founded the sea-crowned city of Gyrton.
146. Iphitus in turn followed with them, the son of Naubolus.
147. who was lord over Phocis and well‑towered Tanagra.
148. Laodokos, Talaos, and Areios, blameless sons,
149. came, the renowned Abantiads, whom Pêrô bore.
150. And Iphidamas, son of Aleus, came; for his stalwart sire sent him forth
151. after leaving the bounds of Tegea.
152. And Erginus came, leaving the grain‑rich ploughland of Branchus,
153. and the turreted citadel of well‑fortified Miletus,
154. where the streams of much‑wandering Maeander wash about.
155. And among them Neleïan Periklymenos arrived,
156. close by Pellene and well-watered Lipaxos,
157. having left the wealthy city and the mountain‑pastured Kolonai.
158. And, leaving Kalydon, swift Meleagros set out.
159. Oineus was his sire, and Althaia, rosy‑armed, bore him.
160. Iphiklos in turn crossed over, leaving the Atracian lake,
161. a kinsman of Althaia; and he honored, above all others, the fair‑formed Meleagros,
162. and he taught him glorious deeds.
163. And Asterion crossed over, the son of renowned Komētus,
164. who dwelt in Peiresiae by the streams of the Apidanus;
165. Peneus, mingling them, bears their common current into the sea.
166. And Eurydamas set forth, leaving Lake Boibeïs,
167. near the Peneios and fair‑open‑sea’d Meliboia.
168. But then the son of Elatos, Polyphemos, arrived,
169. who among the Heroes was preeminent in manly prowess;
170. Aineios, son of Kaineus, arrived, whom they say
171. when he joined with the Lapiths, was subdued by the Centaurs,
172. being struck with pines and with long‑needled firs,
173. and that he endured and planted his unbending knees,
174. and, alive among the dead, went down beneath the depths of the earth.
175. And Admetos came from Pherai, to whom once Paian
176. —serving for hire—stooped; and he was shunning the wrath of Zeus,
177. because indeed the Kyklopes with irresistible arrows
178. he smote among mortals, on account of the wrong done to Asklepios.
179. And there came Eurytion, son of Iros, son of Aktor,
180. leaving rugged Opoeis; and with him came Idas
181. and Lynkeus, who beyond all through the aether and the sea
182. the depths, and the streams of chthonic Ploutes,
183. he alone among men with dread eyes could behold.
184. But when Telamon had joined the company, him she bore
185. to Aiakos the unwearied, the maiden of famed Asopos,
186. Aegina, amid the pebbles of sea‑wreathed Salamis.
187. Then indeed came the stalwart Idmon, bastard son of Abas,
188. whom, having conceived, she bore to lord Apollon
189. beside the stream of Amphrysus—Pheretian Antianeira.
190. To him also Phoibos bestowed prophetic skill and a heaven‑ordained oracular voice,
191. so that he might speak to men words well‑fitted.
192. And in turn there came among them Menoetius from Opus, a comrade of the Minyae;
193. and after him came godlike Oïleus.
194. And far-famed Phlias came, whom once to Bakkhos
195. a Nymph, yielding herself by the streams of Asopos, bore,
196. possessing an unblemished frame and prudent counsel.
197. And Cepheus too, from Arkadia, came among the heroes.
198. But Alcaeus, into the band, from sheep‑abounding Arcadia,
199. his aged father sent him forth for the voyage to the Axenos;
200. he never cast a cloak upon his sturdy shoulders,
201. but about his breast he wrapped the shaggy hide of a bear.
202. Nauplius in turn came, the beloved son of Amymone,
203. whom she bore after sharing the bed with far-famed Ennosigaios,
204. glorious in manly prowess, his form like unto the immortals.
205. And Tainarian Euphemus went forth from the headland of Malea,
206. having left behind the glens and the sea‑surge‑washed precincts.
207. Ankaios too came, the Pleuronian, who indeed had learned the heavenly courses of the stars,
208. and the wandering circuits.
209. For he was inquiring into both the things that are and the things that shall be for mortals.
210. And among them came Palaimonius, the illegitimate son of Lernos;
211. and his two ankle-bones were afflicted, and his feet were not well set;
212. for this cause all were wont to call him the offspring of Hephaistos.
213. And Augeias came, leaving the Pisatid banks of Alpheios,
214. the son of fire-bright Helios.
215. Aye, indeed, two blameless scions came as well,
216. Amphion, famed-of-report, and Asterios, staunch in battle,
217. after leaving Pellene and the haunts of their native land.
218. And again I espied the fair twin ear of Boreas,
219. whom renowned Oreithyia, daughter of divine Erechtheus, bore,
220. having mingled in love beside the flow of the god Ilissos;
221. who indeed had taken to flight with winglets at their ankles,
222. Zetes and Kalais, in form like unto the immortals.
223. But then there came from Pherae a kinsman of lord Pelias;
224. for he had come to be on board the ship Argo,
225. to drive across the Axeinos toward Phasis together with the heroes.
226. And with him there came a comrade of divine Herakles.
227. the fair Hylas; over his dewy chin not yet
228. had the soft first-down reddened his bright cheeks,
229. but he was still a boy, and he greatly pleased Herakles.
230. These men, for their part, assembled at the ship and went into ambush.
231. And then from every side one after another would call to him and speak.
232. And they were preparing the feasts of the all‑hospitable board,
233. But when the heart had had its fill of food and of drink,
234. sitting in order, each man longed for the great enterprise.
235. And rising up all together from the deep sand,
236. they went to where the sea-faring ship was waiting upon the sand,
237. which, as they gazed upon it, they marveled at; but thereafter
238. Argos, with counsels of his mind, made ready the heaving,
239. with wooden rollers and well-twisted cables,
240. having made them fast from the stern. And he called on them to come to the toil,
241. honoring them all. They eagerly obeyed;
242. they stripped off their armor and about their breasts they fastened
243. a rope-thong harness. And each leaned his weight
244. straightway to haul the swift, well-voiced Argō toward the wave.
245. But she, wedged into the sand, had grown heavy for him,
246. being held fast toward the dry land by withered sea‑wrack,
247. unyielding beneath the sturdy palms of the heroes.
248. And Jason’s spirit grew chill again; but to me, indeed,
249. he nodded, casting a glance at me, that for him courage and might
250. I might ever rouse by my song in the wearied.
251. But I, shaking the phorminx in my hands,
252. blended in my mother’s delightful adornment of song,
253. and from my breast I brought forth for them a lily-like voice;
254. “Illustrious Minyan blood of the Heroes’ lineage,
255. come now, set the hawsers beneath your firm breasts
256. bear down in unison, and brace your footprints in the earth,
257. with your soles, stretching far out the extremity of the foot;
258. and, rejoicing, haul the ship toward the gleaming surge.”
259. Argo, pegged with pines and oaks,
260. hearken to my voice; for even before you hearkened,
261. when I was bewitching the trees on a wooded knoll
262. and the pathless rocks, and for my sake you went over the sea,
263. leaving the mountains far behind, and again you followed the
264. virgin tracks of the sea; make haste to reach the Phasis,
265. trusting in my kithara and my god-wrought utterance.”
266. Then the Tomarian oak, booming aloft, obeyed the call,
267. the one which Argos set as the keelson for the black ship, at Pallas’ instigation;
268. and she was very quickly lifted up, lightening her timbers, and swift she slid upon the sea;
269. and as she pressed on she scattered the close-packed rollers
270. which lay beneath her keel, held taut by a single rope.

272. And in she went, and the grim wave of the harbor recoiled.
273. And the sandbanks were washed all around; and Jason’s heart rejoiced.
274. And he leapt inside the ship Argo, and Tiphys followed close at hand.
275. And for him they set the well‑fitted tackle, making all things ready,
276. the mast and the sail‑cloths; and thereon they bound the sheets,
277. fastening them from the stern, and they tightened them with thongs.
278. But then they ran out the oars on either side,
279. and they bade the Minyae to embark in eager haste.
280. Then to them Aisonides spoke winged words:
281. “Hear me, blameless kings; for to me indeed
282. it does not please my heart to lord it over my betters.”
283. But you, whomever your heart and spirit may desire,
284. appoint as leader; and it shall be his to care for all things,
285. to indicate what is to be done, in word and in deed,
286. to those who will sail over the sea and to those who will come to the land,
287. whether to the Colchians or to alien men.
288. For indeed you alone, with me, both many and noble,
289. who even boast yourselves a race from an immortal root,
290. take up the common toil, that you may win glory.
291. But surely I do not suppose it possible to become mightier and more warlike—
292. —than lord Herakles; you yourselves know it as well.
293. Thus he spoke, and they all approved; and with one voice
294. the people roared aloud, that there be an overlord over the Minyai,
295. Alkeidēs, who was by far preeminent among all the comrades.
296. But they did not persuade the prudent lord, who indeed knew
297. the son of Aison to be honored by Hera’s counsels,
298. inasmuch as she was bestowing on him noble renown among those yet to come;
299. for he himself declared that Iason should be commander
300. of the fifty oarsmen, over the dry and the wet.
301. And then indeed all greatly approved, as Herakles bade,
302. and they appointed Iason to be commander.
303. But when Helios, cleaving the boundless aether,
304. with his swift-footed horses was bringing on black Night,
305. then in his breast the son of Aison was devising
306. to establish a pledge among the heroes and oaths of compacts,
307. so that, guarding them, they might trust that all would stand fast;
308. and then, Musaeus, dear child of Antiophemus,
309. he bade me swiftly make ready goodly victims.
310. But I brought to the pebbly strands
311. the timbers, those from the life‑bearing oak; and on top
312. I set in place upon a peplos sea‑washed gifts for the gods.
313. And then indeed a very large krater for the cattle—
314. they slaughtered the bull, having tilted back its head toward the bright aether,
315. while still alive; and around the fire they were pouring the blood, here and there.
316. But when, having crushed the heart, I placed it with popanoi,
317. having poured liquid oil, and thereon the rennet of a lamb.
318. And I bade the heroes, thronging on every side,
319. to plant their spears and their hilted swords,
320. with the hide and the entrails braced in their hands.
321. Then I set in the midst a vessel for kykeon, having set it firm,
322. earthenware, wherein all things had been carefully mixed,
323. first, Demeter’s life-bringing barley-groats,
324. and, upon it, the blood of a bull, and the briny water of the sea.
325. And I bade them wreathe themselves with circles of sacred olive.
326. Then, taking a golden bowl in my own hands,
327. and filling it with kykeon, I apportioned it in succession,
328. for each man among the very-mighty kings to taste.
329. And at the beacon-fire they bade Jason set a torch of dry pine.
330. And under it there ran a wondrous flame.
331. Then indeed I, toward the surge of the loud-roaring sea,
332. stretching out my hands, uttered these words from my tongue,
333. “Guardians of Okeanos and of the brine-swollen sea, deep-dwelling blessed ones,
334. and all you who dwell along the sandy shores, sea-pebbled,
335. and upon Tethys’ utmost water.”
336. Nereus first of all I invoke, the eldest of them all,
337. together with all his fifty lovely maidens;
338. and the glaucous, fish‑swarming sea, and boundless Amphitritê,
339. Proteus and Phorkys, and Triton of broad might,
340. and the nimble winds, together with the golden‑ankled Aurai,
341. and the far‑shining stars, and the mist of murky Night,
342. and the beam of Hêlios, the guide that runs before the feet of his horses,
343. and the sea‑daimones who mingle with heroes,
344. and the shore‑dwelling gods, and the briny streams of the rivers;
345. and himself, the Kronidês, the Earth‑shaker, dark‑haired,
346. to come, leaping forth from the wave, as a helper of oaths.
347. So then, as helpers of Iason, ever steadfast,
348. let us remain willingly, aid-bringers to our shared labors,
349. and let each return alive to his own halls;
350. but whoever shall harm the compact, recking not,
351. by overstepping the oath—of this let witnesses be
352. straight-steering Dike and the dread-giving Erinyes.”
353. Thus I spoke; and they in turn nodded with one mind,
354. fearing the oaths, and signaled with their hands.
355. But when indeed they had sworn and brought the oath to its fulfillment,
356. then at that very time they passed into the hollow hull of their own ship,
357. one after another, and beneath the benches they stowed their gear,
358. setting their hands to the oars; and thereupon Tiphys called out
359. to make fast with the outspread tackle along the long ladder,
360. to unfurl the sails, and to cast off the moorings from the harbor.
361. And then indeed she sent forth a fair, whistling breeze for sailing.
362. Hera, consort of Zeus; and Argo hastened to the voyage.
363. Then they kept both hands and mind upon the rowing,
364. the unwearied kings, and the measureless brine was being cloven,
365. as the foam swelled beneath the keel, on this side and on that.
366. Then holy dawn from the streams of Okeanos
367. was opening the East, and Erigeneia attended,
368. bringing sweet light to mortals and immortals;
369. and then indeed the lookout-heights and the wind‑swept hill
370. from the strand of woody Pelion came into view.
371. But Tiphys, taking his two hands off the steering-ropes,
372. called for them to furrow the wave a little with under-strokes;
373. and swiftly they ran in under the shores;
374. and from the ship they lowered a wooden ladder within the haven.
375. Forth went the Minyan heroes, and they ceased from toil.
376. And among them, in assembly, Peleus the horseman began to speak words:
377. “Friends, look upon the projecting hill of the lookout,
378. shaded in the midst of the headland; here Cheiron
379. dwells in a cave, the most righteous of the Centaurs
380. who were reared on Pholoē and the lofty crests of Pindos,
381. who indeed is devoted to civic justice and to remedies for diseases;
382. and at other times again, striking in his hands the kithara of Phoibos,
383. or the clear‑toned tortoise‑shell phorminx of Hermes,
384. he makes manifest to all the dwellers round about the ordinances of civic justice.”
385. Meanwhile too, silver-footed Thetis our boy,
386. an infant, newly born, took in her arms,
387. went to leaf-shaking Pelion and gave him to Cheiron,
388. to cherish well and skilfully and to rear.
389. Whom indeed a longing is upon my heart to behold in wonder.
390. But, friends, let us draw near to the cave, that I may behold
391. the condition of my own child, and the dispositions wherein he excels.”
392. So speaking he reached the pathless track; and we followed him;
393. and then we entered into the misty courtyard,
394. and there, reclining upon an earthen couch,
395. lay the great Kentauros, propped against a rock,
396. having stretched out his swift horse-limbs.
397. And close at hand stood the son of Thetis and Peleus,
398. with his hand he kept striking the lyre, and Cheiron rejoiced in his heart.
399. But when indeed he beheld the illustrious kings,
400. gladly he sprang up, and he kissed each man;
401. he made ready a feast, and brought wine in amphorae,
402. and he strewed leaves beneath the rough pallets;
403. and he bade them recline beneath unadorned planks,
404. and he set forth in heaps the meats of swine and of swift deer;
405. and thereafter he apportioned the draught of honey-sweet wine.
406. But when at last their spirit was sated with supper and with drink,
407. clapping with their hands they shouted in unison, until I myself
408. should vie with Cheiron, playing the kithara in a most clamorous strain.
409. But I did not obey; for a reverent shame came over me,
410. at, being the younger, matching myself as an equal with an elder;
411. even Cheiron himself kept earnestly entreating me, and, though I was unwilling,
412. he bade me, as he sang, to engage in rivalry for the sake of song.
413. First then the Centaur took up the fair pektis,
414. which Achilles then, bearing it in his hands, held forth.
415. Who then sang of the battle of the storm‑spirited Centaurs,
416. whom the Lapiths slew on account of their own recklessness,
417. and how, raging against Herakles, they fought in Pholoë,
418. since the might of wine had roused them.
419. But I myself, after him, taking up the clear-toned phorminx,
420. launching from my mouth honey-sweet song, I sent it up,
421. First, a dark-voiced hymn of primordial Chaos,
422. how it transmuted the natures, and how heaven came to its bound;
423. and the birth of broad‑bosomed Earth, and the sea’s foundations;
424. and Eros, eldest and self‑complete, of much counsel,
425. all that he brought to be, and how he set one apart from another;
426. and Kronos the dread‑destroyer, and how unto Zeus the Thunder‑delighter
427. came the kingly rule of the blessed immortals.
428. Celebrating the birth and the judgment of the younger Blessed Ones;
429. and the baleful deeds of Brimo, of Bakchos, and of the Giants,

435. And then the trees, root and all, were leaping toward the fold,
436. and the rocks were ringing; and the beasts, hearing the song,
437. remained before the cavern, shying away;
438. and the birds were circling the Centaur’s cattle-stalls,
439. with their feet worn out, and they forgot their own nest.

472. in goodly Lemnos; there indeed evil deeds preoccupied the women,

474. and she, famed Hypsipyle,

526. Straightway the Minyae stepped within the hollow ship.
527. Well-equipped, each took his seat at the thwart.
528. Then Tiphys from the stern raised a shout and gave the order

537. and the far-shining stars were sinking beneath the stream of Oceanos,

542. “You sleep, Agniades, smitten by sweet sleep,
543. having cast a stupor upon your eyelids; but awake, Tiphys,

545. springing forth from the ship, where a stranger upon the sands

548. to be given to those beneath the earth, and to let tears fall from your eyes,
549. in reverence for Themis of pure rites and the table of guest-friendship;

559. and with a shout he roused the host, who were sleeping along the walls here and there, leaning sidelong,

593. a golden sandal, indeed, with pinions outspread.
594. The combat ceased, and tidings flew within the palace.
595. that Kyzikos was no more; and when his ill‑fated wedded consort heard it,

618. But when at last with sacrifices and with prayers we had supplicated,

622. stepping over the bench, and made ready the oar.

624. and the sheets were spread,
625. and swiftly from the peak of Dindymos bright‑veiled Rhea sent a favoring breeze.

629. But when the blast had filled the vessel’s sails,

632. and straightway, racing on, she passed the outflows of Ryndakos,

658. But on the next day we crossed to the baleful land,

676. and they deprived him of the light of day.
677. But when vehement Boreas, with whirling squalls,

679. of Bistonia, so that he might bring on baleful death and doom.
680. But when we had left the lodging of the Agenorid Phineus,

682. which once my mother

684. For there is for him no escape from woeful toil,
685. but, hemmed in by the bright squalls of the winds,

687. and the boom reached over the sea and the broad heaven

694. alone apart from the heroes; but glaukōpis Athēnē

703. eagerly were furrowing the swift waves with their oars;

712. And then indeed, having beforehand escaped the hard-to-avoid woes of destruction,

716. and Sangarios too, which runs its waves upon the Euxeinos.

723. Idmon, Ampycides, and Tiphys the helmsman.

728. in seamanship, pre‑eminent by his own expertise.
729. But he grasped in his hand the rudders’ tiller‑ropes,

731. whom indeed they call, as his by‑name, Kallichoros,

733. Thence, having coasted past a projecting headland, we came
